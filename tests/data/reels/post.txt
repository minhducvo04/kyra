Welcome back to Northwind lecture three. Today we look at the step size in gradient descent. Recall the update rule. We move the parameters opposite the gradient, and the step size scales how far. Here is a concrete example. Take the function f of x equals x squared, and start at x equals four. With a step size of 0.1 the gradient at four is eight, so the update moves us from four to 3.2.

Keep going and the values shrink toward zero. Each step is a bit smaller than the last one. Now watch what happens with a step size of 1.1. The first update takes us from four to negative 4.8. The next one lands at 5.76. The distance from the minimum grows on every step instead of shrinking. That is the surprising part. A larger step does not mean faster progress. Past a threshold it diverges.

A common misconception is that the algorithm is broken. It is not. The step simply overshoots the minimum. The threshold here is a step size of one, because the gradient of x squared is two x and two times one is two. So for this function any step size above one makes each update cross the minimum by more than it came.
